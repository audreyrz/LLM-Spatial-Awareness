In a couple months, in another bare concrete room like this, with a light like this, with faces peering out of dark corners like this, with nerves snapping like this, a body would turn brick red and lunge against the leather straps.

“Who was the partner?” asked Miami.

“The partner,” I said slowly, “was the guy who was not on the left wing of the stage the night Coffee was murdered.”

The room was deathly still.

“He was the guy who Ryan asked to take over the p.a. system on the prompt side of the stage for a few minutes.” I could hear the members of the cast suck in their breaths as the fact struck home. I pressed my knee against the buzzer, and stood up. I reached above to the hanging light and turned it off. The room was pitch dark, and hot as hell. “Here he comes now,” I said.

All eyes focused on the door. The door opened, and a dark figure stood silhouetted in the doorway. It hesitated for a second and cautiously stepped inside. I turned the light back on and rammed it toward his face, so it blazed in his eyes.

It was Stanley Clements.

There was an uproar of voices. “My God!” shouted Burnside, his voice blasting out and vibrating through the room, “it’s Betty Anne Beale’s father. Harold Beale!”

For a split second Clements blinked into the blinding light. I moved forward around the desk and toward him. Instinctively his hand darted inside his coat. I made a dive for him and brought him crashing to the floor. Cheenan nailed his wrist to the concrete with his shoe. Reaching over him, I pulled the .38 from his hand. I got to my feet and handed the gun to Cheenan. “That’s probably the gun that shot the cop,” I told him, then I hauled Clements up. I shoved him over behind the desk, and he fell weakly in the chair. He sat there in a daze.

“Meet Stanley Clements,” I said, “also known as Harold Beale, Mr. Forrester, etc., etc. He’s now famous as the murderer of Coffee Stearns, Jefferson, and Ryan.”

Clements kept rubbing his head, trying to clear it. A big bruise was forming where his face had slapped the floor. Gone was the assurance, the drive, the conceit he’d always worn. His graying hair glistened with sweat under the glaring light.

I reached down and shook him. “Goddamnit, say something you bastard!”

“Yes,” he mumbled, “it’s time to ring down the curtain...”

“Stop being so corny,” I said. “Go ahead and say it. Say it without any Shakespeare or anything else. Tell the whole goddamn world you killed Coffee, Jefferson, and Ryan! Say it!”

“Yes, I killed them,” he said. “I’m not sorry about Jefferson or Ryan. I killed them. It’s only about Coffee. I’m sorry about Coffee. I loved her....” He leaned wearily over the desk and buried his head in his arms. His shoulders shook, but he didn’t make a sound.

“You sonovabitch,” I said, “it’s a good thing for this world there aren’t many guys like you who fall in love with a dame. If that’s the way you show it. Now stop the dramatics and answer a few questions. How come you happened to team up with Coffee?”

He raised his face up toward the light. “I met her about five years ago in a honky-tonk club in Jersey,” he said slowly I was directing the floor show. I got to know her. After a while, she told me about a racket she’d dreamed up, and wanted me to work it with her....”

“Keep going,” I told him.

“We worked it good in New York. Then when Golden Girls took to the road, Coffee wanted to come along to find new suckers. I managed to get on the show as assistant stage manager. It was a good spot, because no one ever saw me, I was backstage all the time. And no one ever recognized Coffee with all her gold paint.”

“How come you killed Coffee on the stage... that night?” I prodded him.

“We’d had a big fight,” he said. “She wanted to split up. I’d trusted her with most the money and she was going to keep it. I loved her, too, like the rest of the suckers,” he added. “She started seeing you and I didn’t like it. I was afraid it would spoil our next pitch. But Coffee insisted people in the show were talking about her... and wondering... so she went out with you to keep them quiet. She didn’t know who you were, and I didn’t either.” He paused and asked for a cigarette. I handed him one and lit it. He took a long drag.

“All that week you were seeing her,” he continued, “we had a lot of arguments. I told her that if she married young Haas, I’d kill her. Then one night in your apartment she found out who you were. She came home that night and told me that if I touched her or laid a hand on her, you’d take care of me, that she had you all wrapped up. Then she walked out of the apartment and was gone all night. I don’t know where she went. Probably she stayed at some hotel.

“The next day about six in the evening, she called the apartment and asked me to bring some stuff from the apartment downtown to her when I came down. I got sore about her moving out and we started arguing over the phone again. She said she was going to see you that night and tell you I’d been threatening her... and had been annoying her with advances. I asked her if she’d talked about me to you at all... yet. She said she hadn’t, but she was sure as hell going to. So then I pleaded with her not to do any talking until after I’d seen her again. I couldn’t afford to have you suspicious of me in any way.”

“Why not?” I asked.

“I still have an old manslaughter charge against me,” he said. “I was drunk and it was a hit-and-run affair. I jumped my bond to avoid a ten-year sentence.”

“There’s the reason he couldn’t blackmail Coffee,” I told Miami. “See? But she didn’t dare use it, except as a last resort”
